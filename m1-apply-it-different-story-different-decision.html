Part 1 of 4

About this application

Try It helped you map what you bring into leadership. Apply It asks you to notice what happens when those experiences and preferences meet an ambiguous leadership situation.

The purpose is not to find the correct interpretation. It is to practice separating observation from assumption before a judgment becomes a decision.

The leadership situation

You are part of a district team considering department chairs for a new cross-district leadership role.

Jordan is widely respected for strong technical knowledge and thoughtful written work. In leadership meetings, Jordan speaks infrequently, rarely jumps into fast-moving discussion, often sends detailed ideas afterward, and has declined several optional social events.

A colleague says, “Jordan is strong technically, but I am not sure Jordan has the presence for district leadership.”

What you'll do

Work through three decisions. Choose the response closest to what you would actually do. There is no score and no correct answer. After each choice you can read what all four responses assume, what evidence each uses, and what each leaves uncertain.

Nothing here is submitted. Your selections stay in this browser and are never sent anywhere or seen by a facilitator or your cohort. There is nothing to type anywhere in this activity.

Part 2 of 4

Decision 1: What do you know?

All you have so far is the pattern of behavior in meetings and one colleague's concern.

Which statement is closest to your first response?

Choose one.

Response A — Jordan needs to become more outspoken

What this centers
Visible participation, treated as a skill Jordan is missing. It moves straight to development, which is a generous instinct.
What it risks overlooking
The judgment has already been made. Speaking frequency has been converted into leadership capacity without anything establishing the link. It also treats being outspoken as a requirement, when so far it is only a description of how the current meetings run.
What it makes possible
A coaching conversation — aimed at a target no one has yet shown the role needs.

Response B — Jordan may not be interested

What this centers
Informal participation, read as a signal of motivation.
What it risks overlooking
Optional events are optional. Attendance can track caregiving, distance, energy, culture, sobriety, or simple preference, none of which speak to interest in a role. And no one in the scenario has asked Jordan.
What it makes possible
This is the assumption easiest to test. One direct question settles it.

Response C — not enough evidence yet

What this centers
Holding observation and interpretation apart, which is the move this decision is built to practice.
What it risks overlooking
That withholding judgment is a starting point, not an answer. Said once and left there, it can become a way to stay out of a decision you are part of. The team still needs a next step.
What it makes possible
The question that changes the conversation: what does this role actually require, and what have we actually seen?

Response D — defer to the colleague who knows Jordan longer

What this centers
Familiarity as expertise, and longer exposure as better evidence.
What it risks overlooking
Length of contact is not accuracy. Deferring hands the standard to one person's read, and that read is the thing in question. It also removes your judgment from a decision you were asked into.
What it makes possible
The colleague's observations are worth having — as information to examine, not a verdict to adopt.

Feedback focus

Separate observable behavior from the meaning being assigned to it. Speaking frequency, social participation, and processing speed may influence how leadership presence is perceived, but they do not by themselves establish leadership capacity.

Leadership question

What behavior do you instinctively read as evidence that someone is ready to lead?

Part 3 of 4

Decision 2: New information

You learn that Jordan's written recommendations are frequently adopted. Colleagues regularly seek Jordan out for advice. In several meetings, Jordan has been interrupted before finishing a point. Jordan has also shared that time to process before responding leads to stronger contributions.

What do you do next?

Choose one.

Response A — help Jordan speak faster

What this centers
Getting Jordan recognized, working with the room as it is.
What it risks overlooking
The whole adjustment sits with Jordan. It asks Jordan to work against the very thing Jordan says produces stronger contributions, and it leaves the interruptions unaddressed — a behavior belonging to other people in the room.
What it makes possible
Recognition for Jordan, if the norm holds. Nothing changes for whoever is in Jordan's position next year.

Response B — reconsider what presence means, and look at the norms

What this centers
The environment as part of the evidence. Being interrupted is a fact about the meeting, not about Jordan.
What it risks overlooking
That the colleague's concern still needs answering out loud, or this reads as sidestepping it. Broad reexamination can also stall a decision with a deadline attached.
What it makes possible
A change that outlasts this candidacy. Interruption and turn-taking are fixable meeting behaviors, and fixing them changes who gets heard from then on.

Response C — leaders adapt to the dominant culture

What this centers
A real constraint. Leaders do work inside cultures they did not design, and pretending otherwise sets someone up to struggle.
What it risks overlooking
The step from describing the culture to requiring it. Held as a standard, an unexamined norm becomes a gate — and the people it filters out are predictable rather than random.
What it makes possible
An honest account of the environment Jordan would enter. That belongs in the conversation as information, not as a criterion.

Response D — rely only on written work

What this centers
Correcting the bias by removing the contested evidence from the table.
What it risks overlooking
If the role genuinely involves facilitating groups or speaking for the district, you have deleted a real requirement rather than examined it. One narrow view has been swapped for another.
What it makes possible
Clarity about which parts of participation the role truly needs — but only if that question gets asked rather than closed.

Feedback focus

Additional context does not require you to dismiss every concern. It does change what can reasonably be inferred from the original behavior. A stronger response examines both the individual evidence and the environment in which that evidence is being produced.

Leadership question

When does an organizational norm become an invisible test of who appears competent?

Part 4 of 4

Decision 3: The recommendation

You must now advise the team about Jordan's candidacy.

What do you recommend?

Choose one.

Response A — recommend Jordan, the concern was biased

What this centers
Naming that the original read was unsupported, and not making Jordan carry the cost of it.
What it risks overlooking
One quick judgment has been replaced by its mirror image. No other candidate has been compared on the same basis, and proves claims more than the evidence supports. Declaring bias tends to end a conversation rather than change the standard it was using.
What it makes possible
A fair hearing for Jordan. The standard the next candidate meets is still invisible.

Response B — senior leaders should command a room

What this centers
A belief about what leadership looks and sounds like, now stated plainly instead of operating quietly. That is worth something.
What it risks overlooking
The belief has not been checked against the role. Commanding a room describes a style; whether this role requires it is a question with an answer, and the posting either says so or it does not.
What it makes possible
A useful moment, if the next question is where that expectation came from and whose leadership it was formed by.

Response C — make the standard visible, then apply it to everyone

What this centers
The criteria themselves, and applying one set of them to every candidate.
What it risks overlooking
That a competency list written inside the same culture can carry the same preferences in more formal language, so the list needs reading too. Run as paperwork after the decision is made, this changes nothing.
What it makes possible
A recommendation you could explain to Jordan, to the colleague who raised the concern, and to the next candidate — and a standard that outlasts this hire.

Response D — delay until the team agrees on a definition

What this centers
Shared understanding before judgment, and a reluctance to decide on a standard nobody has articulated.
What it risks overlooking
Complete agreement on a word like presence is not reachable, so the condition never clears. Delay is not neutral: candidates wait, the role sits vacant, and the caution can look principled while working as avoidance.
What it makes possible
A bounded version of this does work — agree on the competencies the role requires, which is a smaller and answerable question than defining presence.

Feedback focus

The goal is not to replace one quick judgment with another. It is to make the standard visible, identify what the role truly requires, and use evidence connected to those requirements.
