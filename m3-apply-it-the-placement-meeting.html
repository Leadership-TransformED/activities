About this application

Try It asked you to examine your everyday leadership moves. Apply It asks you to make decisions in a situation where several forms of evidence compete, and where a familiar process may not be as neutral as it first appears.

There is no score. The purpose is to notice what your decision treats as evidence, whose knowledge you count, and what tradeoffs your choice creates.

The leadership situation

You are the principal of a middle school. One seat remains in Advanced Math 7.

A student has strong classwork and consistently high course grades, but scored below the usual placement-test range. The student's parent has written asking for placement in the advanced section. Two staff members recommend against placement, citing concerns about pace and readiness. Another teacher points to the student's sustained classroom performance and persistence.

The current process considers test scores, grades, teacher recommendations, and family requests — but the team has never clearly agreed on how those pieces should be weighted when they conflict.

What you'll do

Work through four leadership decisions. Choose the response closest to what you would actually do. After each choice you can read what all four responses treat as evidence, what each protects, and what each risks overlooking. There is no correct answer and nothing is scored.

Nothing here is submitted. Your selections stay in this browser and are never sent anywhere or seen by a facilitator or your cohort. There is nothing to type — the reflection at the end is yours to think through or write in your own notes.

Part 2 of 6

Decision 1: What do you clarify first?

The team is assembled and the decision is on today's agenda.

Which response is closest to what you would do?

Choose one.

Response A — put it to a staff vote

What this treats as evidence
The team's collective read. It feels fair, it is fast, and it shares the weight of a hard call.
What it protects
Staff ownership of the decision, and your own position if it is questioned later.
What it risks overlooking
A vote counts opinions without asking what they rest on. With two staff already recommending against, the outcome is largely set before anyone votes — and the criteria are still unstated, so the majority's habits quietly become the standard.

Response B — clarify the criteria and name where judgment enters

What this treats as evidence
The process itself, examined before any student is discussed.
What it protects
Every student who comes through this process after today, and the staff, who are currently being asked to judge against a standard no one has written down.
What it risks overlooking
It takes meeting time, and can read as stalling to people who came ready to decide. Naming the criteria also does not yet ask whether the criteria are the right ones — that is a further question.

Response C — the test score, as the most objective measure

What this treats as evidence
A single comparable number, on the reasoning that standardized means neutral.
What it protects
Consistency you can defend on paper, and the meeting from a long argument.
What it risks overlooking
An instrument being standardized is not the same as the decision being neutral. Someone chose the cut score, the date, the format, and the conditions. Choosing the test as the tiebreaker is itself professional judgment — just judgment that leaves no fingerprints.

Response D — place the student because the parent asked

What this treats as evidence
The family's knowledge of their own child, which is real evidence and is often discounted.
What it protects
This family's trust, and the student's access to the seat.
What it risks overlooking
It answers the request rather than the question, and none of what the team knows has been weighed. A process that moves for whoever asks favors families who know it can be asked — which is not the same as responding to families.

Leadership question

Where does human judgment enter a process that people describe as objective?

Part 3 of 6

Decision 2: Whose evidence counts?

You learn that the student's classroom work includes sustained success on complex tasks, and that the placement test was completed on a day the student arrived late after a transportation disruption.

What do you do with that?

Choose one.

Response A — the test is standardized, so it stands

What this treats as evidence
Comparability across students, on the principle that fairness means one measure applied the same way to everyone.
What it protects
The appearance of even-handedness, and the team from having to weigh anything.
What it risks overlooking
What you just learned. A late arrival after a transportation disruption is information about the measurement, not an excuse for the student — the standardized conditions were not standard that morning. Holding the score as strongest now requires ignoring a known problem with it.

Response B — set the test aside, rely on grades

What this treats as evidence
Sustained performance over months, rather than one sitting.
What it protects
The student from a single bad morning, and the daily evidence teachers actually see.
What it risks overlooking
Grades vary by teacher and by how much rigor sits behind them, so they need the same scrutiny. Discarding a measure entirely is the mirror image of over-weighting it, and the test may still say something useful about specific skills worth supporting.

Response C — several sources, each tied to specific skills

What this treats as evidence
Each source as evidence about something particular, including the conditions the test was taken under — and the student, who is the only person in the situation nobody has asked.
What it protects
Both rigor and access. It can name what the student can already do and what support the course would require.
What it risks overlooking
It takes longer, and "multiple measures" becomes cover if nobody says which skills the course actually demands. Gathering can also substitute for deciding, and the seat is being decided today.

Response D — ask the parent to decide

What this treats as evidence
Family authority over the child's path, which sounds respectful and defers to the person who knows the student longest.
What it protects
You, mostly. The decision and its consequences move across the table.
What it risks overlooking
It hands a professional judgment to someone without the professional information, and if the placement goes badly the family carries the blame for it. Asking a parent to choose between their child's opportunity and their child's struggle is not a real choice. Family perspective belongs in the decision, not as the decision.

Leadership question

What becomes visible when context is treated as relevant evidence rather than as an excuse?

Decision 3: The meeting dynamic

During the discussion, one staff member says:

“Families always think their child should be in the advanced class.”

The parent is not present.

What do you do?

Choose one.

Response A — let it pass

What this treats as evidence
That the comment is a side remark rather than part of the deliberation.
What it protects
Momentum, the relationship with that staff member, and a decision that is due today.
What it risks overlooking
Silence from the person with authority reads as agreement. The generalization stays in the room and shapes how this parent's request gets weighed, and the rest of the team learns that remarks like it are fine here.

Response B — redirect to evidence, name the generalization

What this treats as evidence
This family's actual request, rather than a category of families.
What it protects
The absent parent, the norm for the meeting, and the staff member's standing — the comment is addressed without a public reprimand.
What it risks overlooking
Wording carries a lot here; even a light redirect can land as a rebuke in front of colleagues. And a redirect alone may leave the moment unfinished — a private follow-up is often the other half of it.

Response C — end the meeting

What this treats as evidence
The seriousness of the comment, refusing to carry on as though nothing was said.
What it protects
A clear line, unmistakably drawn.
What it risks overlooking
The cost lands on the student and the family, who now wait. It also tends to make the episode about the staff member rather than about the standard, and teams that experience this usually learn to say less rather than to think differently. A comment of this kind can be addressed in the room.

Response D — family advocacy should count for less

What this treats as evidence
Professional expertise as the more reliable source, and a real concern that persistent requests can bend a process.
What it protects
Staff judgment, and consistency against the loudest voice.
What it risks overlooking
It turns the generalization into policy. Discounting advocacy as a class discounts the families with the least access to you as much as the most persistent ones — and it does not solve the actual problem, which is that nobody has said how a family request is weighed for anyone.

Leadership question

What does a leader communicate when a generalization goes unexamined?

Part 5 of 6

Decision 4: The recommendation

The evidence remains mixed. The team must make a decision today.

What do you decide?

Choose one.

Response A — maintain the usual placement

What this treats as evidence
Precedent, and the value of a process people can predict.
What it protects
Against favoritism, and against a decision that looks like it was won by whoever pushed hardest. Not a small thing.
What it risks overlooking
Consistency with a process the team has just agreed has no stated weighting is consistency with a habit. Whatever that habit has been producing, it goes on producing — and this case, which surfaced the gap, teaches the team nothing.

Response B — place the student, no follow-up

What this treats as evidence
The classroom record, and the case for giving the benefit of the doubt.
What it protects
Access to the seat, today.
What it risks overlooking
The whole risk now sits with the student. No supports, no agreed evidence to look at, no one scheduled to notice. If it goes badly, it becomes a story the school tells about students like this one for years — and the story will blame the student rather than the plan.

Response C — time-limited, supported, with a check-in

What this treats as evidence
What is known now, plus what the next few weeks would show — and it says in advance what would count.
What it protects
Rigor and access at once, and it leaves a record the team can use the next time evidence conflicts. That is how a process actually changes.
What it risks overlooking
It only works if someone owns the follow-through; an unheld check-in becomes either a permanent placement nobody examined or a quiet removal. And "time-limited" can land on a student and family as probation, so how it is said matters as much as what it is.

Response D — delay until the information is complete

What this treats as evidence
The gaps. It takes seriously that the picture is incomplete.
What it protects
The team from deciding badly under pressure.
What it risks overlooking
Perfect information does not arrive, and the seat and the schedule do not wait for it. Delay is itself a decision — it just is not recorded as one, and it resolves as the usual placement by default. A short, bounded pause for one specific missing piece is a different thing, and defensible.

Leadership question

How do you preserve rigor while refusing to confuse one narrow measure with the full picture of readiness?
